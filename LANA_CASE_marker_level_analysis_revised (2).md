# LANA-CASE: Marker-Level Functional Analysis and Cross-Marker Comparison

## 1. Analytical focus

The analysis examines the markers from three connected perspectives:

1. **Epistemic meaning:** how markers express knowledge, lack of knowledge, probability, inference, and degrees of commitment.
2. **Speaker stance:** how speakers present a personal view, assessment, expectation, or interpretation.
3. **Interactional function:** how markers contribute to the management of interaction, including the presentation of a position and the handling of alignment or disagreement.

A marker should not be interpreted independently of its context. Its function may vary across interactional situations.

## 2. Individual marker analysis

### 2.1. *I think*

*I think* is used to express a speaker's personal view or assessment. It can also mark an inference and contribute to the management of interaction by presenting the speaker's own position or evaluation.

### 2.2. *I guess*

*I guess* is used to express a personal impression or a cautious assessment. Like *I think*, it can introduce a personal view, but it presents that view more tentatively. In some interactional contexts, *I guess* can also function as a form of indirect agreement.

### 2.3. *I know*

*I know* expresses knowledge and assurance. Interactionally, it can emphasize the speaker's epistemic position and contribute to alignment or disagreement, depending on the context.

### 2.4. *I don't know*

*I don't know* expresses a lack or limitation of knowledge. Its function is not restricted to communicating that the speaker lacks information; it can also play an interactional role in managing the relationship with the interlocutor.

### 2.5. *I thought*

*I thought* presents a belief, expectation, or interpretation from a past perspective. This past-oriented perspective is relevant to how the speaker presents an earlier position or expectation.

### 2.6. *Maybe*

*Maybe* expresses possibility and uncertainty. It is related to *might* and *could*, although the three forms are not interchangeable.

### 2.7. *Might*

*Might* presents a possible event or state and is associated with a relatively low degree of epistemic commitment. It opens up a possibility without presenting it as certain.

### 2.8. *Could*

*Could* is used to present possibility and is considered alongside *maybe* and *might*. These forms differ in how they present possibility or inference.

### 2.9. *Probably*

*Probably* presents a likely conclusion and is associated with inferential assessment. It is compared with *must* as a marker used in drawing an inference.

### 2.10. *Must*

*Must* can express an inference or a conclusion drawn from available information. It is compared with *probably*, although the two markers are not treated as equivalent.

## 3. Cross-marker comparisons

### 3.1. *I think* and *I guess*

Both markers can express a personal view. The distinction concerns how that view is presented: *I think* expresses the speaker's view or assessment, whereas *I guess* presents a more tentative impression or cautious assessment. *I guess* may also function as indirect agreement in some interactional contexts.

### 3.2. *I know* and *I don't know*

These markers contrast in terms of the speaker's epistemic position:

- *I know* presents knowledge or assurance and can emphasize the speaker's epistemic position.
- *I don't know* presents a lack or limitation of knowledge, while also potentially performing interactional work with respect to the interlocutor.

The contrast is therefore not limited to positive versus negative wording; the markers also have different interactional potential.

### 3.3. *Maybe*, *might*, and *could*

These forms all relate to possibility, but they should not automatically be treated as equivalent. They differ in how a speaker presents a possible interpretation, event, or state and how the speaker's commitment is conveyed.

### 3.4. *Probably* and *must*

Both markers are associated with inference and the presentation of a conclusion. They represent distinct ways of expressing an inferential assessment.

## 4. Interactional dimension

The analysis identifies interaction as an important dimension alongside epistemic meaning. Relevant dimensions include:

- differences in the degree of certainty or commitment expressed;
- the contrast between expressing probability, knowledge, and lack of knowledge;
- the role of markers in interaction and in managing the exchange;
- variation in marker use across different contexts.

In particular, *I know*, *I don't know*, *I guess*, and *I think* can contribute to interaction in different ways. *I know* may emphasize an epistemic position and participate in alignment or disagreement; *I don't know* may help manage the relationship with the interlocutor; and *I guess* may, in some contexts, support indirect agreement.

These functions are context-sensitive and do not necessarily apply to every occurrence.

## 5. General analytical observation

The markers should be interpreted in context. Their functions cannot be determined solely from the marker form: the surrounding discourse and the interactional situation also matter. The analysis therefore distinguishes between a marker's broad epistemic contribution and the particular function it performs in an individual use.

## 6. Scope
