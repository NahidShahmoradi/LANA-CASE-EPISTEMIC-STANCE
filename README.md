# Epistemic Stance in American Spoken English

A corpus-based pilot study using LANA-CASE

This repository documents an independent corpus-based pilot study investigating how selected lexical and grammatical expressions are used to convey epistemic stance in spontaneous American English conversation.


## Project overview

The project examines a set of ten target expressions in the Lancaster–Northern Arizona Corpus of American Spoken English (LANA-CASE). It combines contextual manual analysis with a structured annotation framework to explore how speakers express knowledge, uncertainty, inference, possibility, and related interpersonal or interactional meanings.

The analysis is designed to be sensitive to context: the same expression may perform different functions across conversational environments. Accordingly, the project does not assume that each marker has one fixed meaning or that epistemic commitment can always be represented on a single numerical scale.


## Research focus

The project focuses on the following ten expressions:

- I think

- I know

- I don’t know

- maybe

- probably

- I guess

- could

- might

- must

- I thought


## Research questions

1. What epistemic and discourse-interactional functions do the selected expressions perform in spontaneous American English conversation?

1. How do the expressions differ in their typical contextual uses and interactional roles?

1. How can contextual manual annotation capture similarities and contrasts among expressions without collapsing distinct analytical dimensions?


## Corpus and data

The project draws on LANA-CASE, a corpus of contemporary spoken American English. The corpus contains approximately 9 million tokens across 1,477 texts and 1,116 speakers, with conversations collected between 2022 and 2024. The corpus was accessed through #LancsBox X under the corpus user licence.

The current working dataset consists of 100 marker-oriented sample records selected for contextual analysis. Of these, 93 are currently treated as core-eligible for the epistemic analysis: 53 confirmed cases and 40 provisional cases. Records excluded from the core analysis are retained in the internal working data where relevant for auditability and as non-epistemic comparisons. These figures describe the present analytical sample and should not be interpreted as a representative or random sample of the entire corpus.


## Analytical framework

The project keeps two analytical layers distinct: (1) epistemic stance/function coding, which concerns the contribution of an expression in its local context; and (2) interactional-response coding, which captures how an utterance relates to the preceding conversational move. These layers are not treated as interchangeable.

Broad stance-family labels are used as provisional organisational categories rather than as a final theoretical taxonomy. Ambiguous, unmapped, low-confidence, and provisional cases are retained as such instead of being forced into a definitive category. No universal numerical certainty scale is imposed.


## Method at a glance

1. Identify occurrences of the ten target expressions in the selected corpus material.

1. Examine each occurrence in its local conversational context.

1. Apply contextual manual coding for epistemic stance/function.

1. Code interactional responses separately from epistemic stance.

1. Reconcile annotations while preserving uncertainty flags and non-epistemic exclusions.

1. Compare patterns across target expressions and report aggregate findings.


## Current project status

The contextual review and manual validation of the initial 100-sample set have been completed. A marker-level synthesis has also been prepared. The token-level inventory is a separate first-pass extraction aid and still requires occurrence-level validation; it should not be read as a completed token-level functional analysis.


## Preliminary findings

Descriptive results from the 93 core records of the pilot sample (full tables in FINDINGS.md):

- Some markers map closely onto a single function: *I thought* (9 of 9 records) onto past belief or expectation, *might* (9 of 9) and *could* (8 of 9) onto possibility, and *probably* (7 of 10) onto probability.

- *I know* was mainly interactional in this sample: 8 of 10 records were labelled acknowledgment or alignment, and only 1 as knowledge.

- *I guess* and *I think* were the most functionally varied markers, with *I guess* receiving six different labels across ten records.

- *Must* had the highest rate of non-epistemic use: 4 of its 10 records were excluded as necessity or intention.

These counts describe an exploratory, marker-oriented sample and are not estimates of corpus-wide frequency. Validation is uneven across markers, and results for *I think*, *I guess* and *I know* rest mostly on provisional labels.

## Data access, licence, and responsible use

LANA-CASE is made available under a user licence for non-commercial research and teaching. The corpus is not public domain. The licence restricts redistribution of corpus materials and modified corpus data, and also places restrictions on external cloud storage and on uploading corpus transcripts to AI services that retain submitted data.

For this reason, this public repository will not include raw corpus files, audio, transcripts, speaker-identifying information, or record-linked annotated exports unless the corpus rights holders have explicitly authorised their release in writing. Public-facing outputs will be limited to project documentation, original code that does not reproduce corpus content, and aggregate results that are permissible to share under the licence. Users should consult the official corpus page and licence before attempting to access or reuse the corpus.
